# Check what it tells you

This step is where a reply stops being the tool's and becomes yours. Before any figure, rule or recommendation from an AI assistant shapes a decision, you take it outside the tool and test it against something that does not compose its answers. The step is short when the question was asked well, because the reply already names what needs checking.

## Why it matters

The tool cannot check itself. Asking it whether it is sure produces another composed sentence, and asking it to confirm an answer produces agreement. A [hallucination](/glossary#hallucination) reads exactly like a fact, in the same tone, with the same formatting. Only something outside the tool can tell the two apart, and that something is you, with a source open.

Money answers go wrong in three places: a figure that is wrong or out of date, a rule that is misstated or does not apply to you, and a recommendation built on an assumption you never made. Each failure needs a different check, and none of them takes long once you know which to run. Skipping this step does not save time; it moves the cost to after you have acted.

## What actually happens

### Sort the reply into what can be checked

Read the reply and label each claim. Facts can be checked against a source: a figure, a deadline, a rule, a feature of a product. Reasoning can be followed step by step and either holds or does not. Opinions, such as whether something is a good idea for you, cannot be checked, only weighed. The first two kinds get the checks below; the third goes to the next step, where you decide whether it needs a person.

### Take every figure to a primary source

A figure the tool states is a lead, not a fact. Find it in a [primary source](/glossary#primary-source): the agency's own page, the statute, your lender's agreement, your own statement. Confirm the number and the year it applies to. If the tool cited a source, open it; a citation that does not exist, or does not say what the tool claims, is common and looks identical to a real one. A [secondary source](/glossary#secondary-source) such as a news article can point you to the primary one but does not replace it.

### Check that the rule applies to you

A rule can be stated correctly and still not be yours: a different country or state, a different kind of account, an earlier year, a threshold you are on the other side of. Find the condition in the source and set it against your own facts. The tool fills gaps with the common case, so this check matters most where your situation is not the common one.

### Surface the assumptions

A recommendation rests on [assumptions](/glossary#assumption): a rate of return, how long you hold, that nothing in your life changes. Ask the tool to list them if the reply did not, then change one and see whether the recommendation survives. A conclusion that flips when one assumption moves is a conclusion about the assumption, and the assumption is yours to decide.

### Redo the arithmetic

If the reply contains a calculation, do it again yourself, on a calculator or in a spreadsheet, using the tool's own inputs. The tool is unreliable at arithmetic in a particular way: the answer is plausible, neatly formatted and occasionally wrong, with nothing to mark the wrong ones. Two minutes with the numbers catches what no amount of rereading will.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Which sentences in this reply are facts I can look up, and have I looked them up?
- Did I open the source the tool named, and does it say what the tool said?
- Does the rule it stated actually cover my country, my account and this year?
- What is this recommendation assuming, and what happens if one assumption is wrong?
- Have I done the arithmetic myself, or only read the tool's?

## Common mistakes

- Asking the tool to double-check its own answer and treating the second reply as a check.
- Trusting a citation because it is formatted like one, without opening it.
- Confirming the figure but not the year it applies to.
- Checking the rule exists but not that it applies to you.
- Stopping at the first source that agrees with the tool, which may be repeating the same error.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
A client who is doing this step on their own needs a short list, not a method: open the source, confirm the year, test one assumption, redo the sum. Ask them to bring you the source they found alongside the answer they got; the gap between the two is usually where the conversation needs to go. If they could not find a source at all, that is the finding, and it means the answer does not get acted on. In their file, note which answers they checked and against what, so that when a figure turns out to be wrong you can see whether the check was skipped or the source was stale.
</Callout>
