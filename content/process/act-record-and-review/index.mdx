# Act, record and review

This step is what happens after the checking: you decide whether to act, you write down what you relied on, and later you look back at how it went. It closes the loop. What you learn here about which questions the tool handled well is what you take into deciding what to use it for next time.

## Why it matters

A checked answer is still only information. Whether to act on it is a decision about your own money, and the tool does not make that decision, carry any responsibility for it, or know what it did not ask you. Some decisions are safe to make on a checked answer and some need a person who can take your whole situation into account and be accountable for what they tell you. Knowing which is which is the point of this step.

The record matters because money decisions get revisited: a tax return is queried, a loan is disputed, a plan is reviewed. A short note of what you relied on turns "I asked an AI" into something you can show, and it is what lets the review at the end of this step work. Without the note, you cannot learn from the outcome because you cannot remember what the inputs were.

## What actually happens

### Decide whether this is yours to act on

Sort the decision, not the answer. A checked explanation or a confirmed figure you can act on yourself: setting a reminder for a deadline, choosing between two products whose terms you have read, understanding a letter before you reply. A decision that is large, hard to reverse, tangled with tax or law, or specific to your circumstances in ways a general answer cannot see needs a person. The tool's answer is a good brief to take to that person, and a poor substitute for them.

### Know what advice is and who gives it

[Advice](/glossary#advice) is a recommendation made for your situation by someone who takes responsibility for it, usually under a licence or a professional duty. An answer from an AI assistant is not advice, however personal it sounds, because nobody stands behind it. If the question needs advice, the tool's job is finished once it has helped you understand the question; the answer comes from a person you can name.

### Act in a way you can undo

Where you do act, prefer the reversible step. Pay the extra instalment this month rather than changing the term; move part of a balance rather than all of it; set the limit lower and raise it later. A step you can undo turns a wrong answer into a small cost. Watch for urgency, too: a reply or a message that says you must act today is a reason to slow down, not speed up.

### Write a decision note

Before you act, write a [decision note](/glossary#decision-note): the date, the question you asked, what the tool said, the source you checked it against, and what you decided. Four lines is enough. Keep it with the paperwork the decision belongs to, not in the tool's history, which can be deleted or lost with your account. If a person was involved, note who and what they said.

### Review how it turned out

When the outcome is visible, which may be a month or a year later, reread the note. Was the figure right? Did the rule apply? Did the assumption hold? Did acting on your own turn out fine, or would a person have changed the decision? The answers tell you which kinds of question this tool handled well for you and which it did not, and that goes straight back into the first step of the loop.

## Questions to ask yourself

- Is this a decision I can make on a checked answer, or one a person needs to be accountable for?
- Can I undo this if the answer turns out to be wrong?
- Have I written down what I relied on, where I will find it later?
- Is anything about this pushing me to act faster than I would otherwise?
- When will I know how this turned out, and have I set a time to look?

## Common mistakes

- Treating a confident, personal-sounding answer as advice because it was addressed to you.
- Acting on the whole recommendation at once when a smaller, reversible step would test it.
- Keeping the only record of the decision inside the tool's conversation history.
- Taking the answer to a professional instead of the question, so the conversation starts from the tool's framing.
- Never reviewing, so the same kind of question gets the same kind of mistake next time.

## For practitioners

<Callout type="info">
A client who is doing this step on their own benefits from two things you can give them in a sentence each: the test for what needs you, and the four-line decision note. Ask them to bring the note, not the conversation, when a tool-informed decision comes to you; it tells you what they relied on and where the check happened. If they acted on an unchecked or uncheckable answer, treat it as you would any decision made on a tip, and say so plainly. In their file, record the note they brought, your view of the decision, and anything you corrected, so the pattern across visits is visible to both of you.
</Callout>
